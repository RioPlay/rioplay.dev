I'm RioPlay. I like taking things apart to see what else they can do, and this is where I write about it.

== Where this started

I have been messing with computers for about as long as I can remember. What hooked me was simple: there seemed to be no bottom to them, an almost limitless number of directions to wander off in and learn.

My first real personal computer was an Acer C720 Chromebook, with all of 2 GB of RAM. It was meant to be a browser with a keyboard attached, a locked-down thing that did a short list of approved tasks and nothing else. I spent most of my time with it ignoring that list.

It started modestly. I "dual booted" in the loosest sense of the phrase, chrooting into an Ubuntu instance alongside ChromeOS because that was the easy path back then. I had no idea I was paging to disk as hard as I was on a 2 GB machine. It just felt slow, and I kept going anyway. Then I kept learning. I flipped the device into developer mode, got it booting raw Linux, and started peeling things back to fit the hardware, working down through desktop environments until I landed on the light ones: XFCE, and then LXDE. I am fairly sure it was LXDE, since it was the earlier option at the time. Some of the details are hazy now, it was a long time ago.

The part I will never forget is getting that little 1366x768 panel to run Minecraft at a miraculous 60 frames per second. Somewhere in all of it I picked up the command-line navigation and everyday Linux habits I still lean on, and the whole experience jumpstarted an interest in computers that only grew from there.

That habit never went away. It just moved on to bigger machines and harder problems.

== What I actually do

I am not a developer at my core, and I would rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise. I work in IT. I started out on the service desk and have since moved up the chain. In my own time I run a homelab, which is mostly a license to break things on purpose and learn how they really work.

That background shapes how I build. I come at software the way a sysadmin comes at a broken system: poke it, watch what it does, form a theory, test it, repeat. Troubleshooting is the real skill, and it transfers further than people expect.

== What this site is

This is a personal blog, not a single-topic one. I expect it to wander across whatever I am building, breaking, learning, or curious about: developer tooling, the projects I make, the occasional rabbit hole. The through-line is not a subject, it is the way I like to approach things.

The reason it exists at all is xref:./series/aden/_index.md[*Aden*], a tool I have been building that turns a codebase into a queryable map for humans and AI agents. Aden is the first project I cared enough about to want to write it down and share it as I go, and once I started, a blog felt like the right home for the rest of my interests too.

A couple of corners worth knowing about:

* The xref:./glossary.adoc[Glossary] defines recurring terms once and links out to their sources, so posts can stay readable without re-explaining the basics.
* The xref:./archives.md[Archive] is the full index of everything published.

The site is built with Hugo and written in AsciiDoc, the same plain-text format Aden reads. I liked the idea of writing about the tool on the same substrate the tool is built on.

== A note on AI

Aden was built with heavy AI assistance, and I am not going to bury that. It is one of the more interesting parts of the story.

Getting an AI to help build something genuinely new, instead of a slightly reworded copy of what already exists, is not a matter of asking nicely. It took a lot of direction, correction, and plain stubbornness to bend the tool toward an idea it had no template for, and to hold the result to a standard I was happy to put my name on. The polish is not something the model handed me. It is the part I worked hardest on.

I treat AI as exactly that, a tool, only as useful as the person wielding it. It did not replace me. It took the laborious, time-consuming work off my plate so I could spend my attention on the decisions that actually needed a person. Used that way it is the most leverage I have ever had as one person, and I would rather you see what it let me build than dismiss it for the label.

== What Aden is built on

Aden is a Rust project and it does not exist in a vacuum. The full attribution — all 350+ transitive dependencies, each with its license — is in https://github.com/RioPlay/aden/blob/main/NOTICE.md[NOTICE.md^]. A few names are load-bearing enough to name here.

*Language foundation.* Aden's whole premise — that documentation can be a plain-text, referential, machine-queryable format — rests on https://asciidoc.org[AsciiDoc^], which **Stuart Rackham** created in 2002. The original insight that a document could have a regular grammar, cross-references, and conditionals, and still be readable as text, is exactly what lets Aden treat docs as a graph instead of opaque files. **Dan Allen** and the https://asciidoctor.org[Asciidoctor^] project (and the AsciiDoc Working Group at the Eclipse Foundation) carried that forward into the processor and language spec in use today. This blog is also written in AsciiDoc; the substrate is the same.

*Parsing and search.* **Max Brunsfeld**, the https://tree-sitter.github.io/tree-sitter/[tree-sitter^] project, and the many per-language grammar authors bundled via https://github.com/kreuzberg-dev/tree-sitter-language-pack[tree-sitter-language-pack^] are why Aden can extract call graphs, signatures, and symbols across 300+ languages from a single compiled binary, with no runtime downloads. **Andrew Gallant (BurntSushi)** and contributors — the `regex`, `aho-corasick`, `memchr`, `walkdir`, and `ignore` crates — are what Aden's structure-aware grep, lint, and file discovery are built on.

*Storage, graph, and data.* https://github.com/fjall-rs/fjall[fjall^] (LSM-tree embedded storage), https://github.com/petgraph/petgraph[petgraph^] (graph data structures), and **David Tolnay** and the https://serde.rs[serde^] community (`postcard`, `serde_json`, `serde_yaml`, `toml`). Content-hash freshness tracking is https://github.com/BLAKE3-team/BLAKE3[blake3^].

*Async, parallelism, and CLI.* The https://tokio.rs[Tokio^] team, https://github.com/rayon-rs/rayon[rayon^] for data-parallel indexing, and https://github.com/clap-rs/clap[clap^] for the command interface.

*Graph visualization.* https://github.com/vasturiano/force-graph[force-graph^] and https://github.com/vasturiano/3d-force-graph[3d-force-graph^] by **Vasco Asturiano** (both MIT), bundled fully offline in `aden view`. The interactive graph on this blog's link:/graph/[/graph/] page runs on the same library.

*AI protocol.* https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/rust-sdk[rmcp^], the Rust Model Context Protocol SDK from Anthropic and the MCP community, is how `aden mcp install` wires Aden's commands into an AI client as first-class tools.

*Optional semantic embeddings.* The https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5[BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5^] model (MIT, HuggingFace), with the https://github.com/sonos/tract[tract^] ONNX runtime and HuggingFace https://github.com/huggingface/tokenizers[tokenizers^], power the optional `dense` feature that fuses local embeddings with BM25 for natural-language queries. Fetched on demand, never bundled, fully offline once downloaded.

If you maintain a project Aden depends on and feel under-credited, that is an oversight worth fixing — https://github.com/RioPlay/aden[open an issue^].
